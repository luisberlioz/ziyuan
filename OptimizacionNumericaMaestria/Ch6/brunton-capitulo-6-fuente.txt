Chapter 6

Constraints and Duality

Constraints are an essential component of many optimization problems. Most real-world
problems are constrained by resources, time, material properties, cost, etc. Until now we
have mostly either considered unconstrained problems, such as least-squares regression
and gradient descent, or problems with the simplest constraints and cost functions, such
as linear programming.
     Constraints shape the feasible set of an optimization problem. In Chapter 3 on linear
programming, we saw that the constraints almost entirely determine the problem, as the
optimum always lives on the vertex of the feasible polytope defined by the constraints.
We also saw constraints arise in Chapter 4 in the context of constrained linear regression.
The importance of constraints in optimization extends far beyond these simple linear and
quadratic problems, becoming critical for modeling a wide range of real-world systems.
     We begin this chapter with an in-depth discussion about Lagrange multipliers for
enforcing equality constraints. We will derive the conditions for optimality of a con-
strained problem, with several examples. Next, we formally introduce the more general
Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions, which hold for inequality constraints as well. La-
grange multipliers and the KKT conditions have an intuitive geometric interpretation in
terms of directional derivatives of the objective and constraint functions. Specifically, lo-
cally optimal solutions will be those such that the gradient of the objective function has
no direction tangent to the constraint equations. Thus, the objective normal must be in the
span of the constraint normal directions, and the linear combination of these constraint
normals will be the Lagrange multipliers.
     The interactions between constraints and the objective function are surprisingly rich
and interesting. The concept of duality provides crucial insight into this connection. At
its core, duality provides a deeper perspective on constrained optimization, often by refor-
mulating the original, primal problem into a complementary dual problem. Analyzing this
dual problem can reveal tight bounds on the original objective and provide methods for
efficient computation. In some cases, the dual formulation may be simpler or more com-
putationally tractable. We will explore duality and constraints, expanding and developing
the earlier perspective in Section 1.4 based on infinite penalty functions. Dual variables
also have the useful interpretation as the sensitivities of the objective function with respect
to active constraints.
     Duality is most easily understood through linear programming (via the primal-dual
pair of linear programs) and quadratic programming (via the classical use of Lagrange

multipliers and KKT conditions). However, the concept of duality is much more widely
applicable. In mechanics and physics, for instance, dual principles connect variational for-
mulations of equilibrium conditions with complementary energy or stress-based descrip-
tions. In the calculus of variations, dual arguments can simplify or solve certain boundary
value problems by re-expressing constraints or re-labelling the roles of independent and
dependent variables.
    Duality then lead us to interior point methods (IPMs), which are a powerful class of
algorithms for constrained optimization. There are several variants of IPMs that leverage
primal and dual formulations to efficiently find optimal solutions to convex problems.
These methods may also be used for nonconvex problems, as were discussed in Chapter 5,
although there are typically fewer guarantees in the nonconvex case, and extra care must
be taken.
    Next, we introduce one of the most widely used splitting algorithms, the alternating
direction method of multipliers (ADMM). ADMM is a powerful primal-dual method that
is used to handle complex problems with nonsmooth regularizers. ADMM relies on the
proximal operator from Sec. 5.1, and is closely related to the sparse, relaxed, regularized
regression from the same section. Both methods involve operator splitting and proximal
steps; however, SR3 is primal only, while ADMM is primal-dual. This introduces subtle
differences that we will discuss throughout this section.
    Finally, we will introduce infinite dimensional optimization problems and the calculus
of variations. This is a rich field with an equally rich history. Although it may seem beyond
the scope of this chapter, the concepts of constraints and duality were first introduced
in these infinite-dimensional problems in the context of classical mechanics. The Euler-
Lagrange equations, which are used to derive the equations of motion in physics, also
provide optimality conditions for infinite-dimensional optimization problems. Further,
the Legendre transformation is an important transformation of the Lagrangian into a dual
Hamiltonian formulation.
    Variational optimization is extremely relevant for broad and useful classes of infinite
dimensional problems, including control theory, machine learning, and shape optimiza-
tion in fluid applications. We will investigate several key infinite dimensional optimiza-
tion problems, such as the classic Brachistochrone and Tautochrone problems, which have
broad implications for modern optimization problems. We already saw a very simplified
cousin of the Brachistochrone problem in Example Panel 1.2 in Chapter 1, and we will
also explore how to solve the full Brachistochrone problem using modern techniques in
automatic differentiation in Chapter 10.
    To summarize, this chapter will accomplish three major goals:

  1. Describe how to handle constraints, and how they may be tractably optimized through
     Lagrange multipliers and KKT conditions;

  2. Introduce the related concept of duality, which is important in advanced algorithms,
     such as IPMs, discussed in Sec. 6.4, and ADMM, discussed in Sec. 6.5;

  3. Discuss infinite-dimensional problems where the objective is a functional of a func-
     tion to be optimized.

6.1      Lagrange Multipliers
We begin by discussing equality constraints h(x) = 0, which are the simplest class of
constraints to handle. The constrained optimization problem is written as

                                                 min f (x),                                              (6.1a)
                                                  x
                                                 s.t. h(x) = 0.                                          (6.1b)

    The method of Lagrange multipliers reformulates the constrained optimization prob-
lem, moving the constraint equations into the objective function. This introduces addi-
tional decision variables, the Lagrange multipliers λ, that act as regularization weights to
enforce the constraint:

                                        min sup       f (x) + λT h(x).                                     (6.2)
                                         x       λ

This form of the objective function L(x, λ) = f (x) + λT h(x) is known as the Lagrangian.
This is closely related to the Lagrangian from classical mechanics (Sec. 6.6) and control
theory (Chapter 9). Now, in addition to minimizing over x, we are also extremizing over
λ in the inner supremum1 .
     We derived a simplified form of this in Section 1.4 using the infinite penalty function
δ0 (h(x)), which we added to the objective function; this function is zero when the argu-
ment is equal to zero and infinite when the argument is not. If we add this penalty function
to the objective function

                                          min f (x) + δ0 (h(x))

then the optimal solution is equivalent to that of the original constrained problem. We
then replace this nonsmooth term with λT h(x), and as λj goes to infinity, this term is large
enough to enforce the hj (x) = 0 constraint. Writing minx supλ takes care of this detail, as it
ensures that when the constraint is violated the inner supremum is infinite, and when the
constraint is satisfied, it reduces to the original constrained problem in Eq. (6.1).

Panel 6.1: Minimax Inequality
   To derive the general form of the minimax inequality, which we used in Eq. (6.3),
   we let f : X × Y → R be any real-valued function. Then we have

                             min max     f (x, y)    ≥    max min      f (x, y).
                             x∈X y∈Y                       y∈Y x∈X

   This inequality always holds, regardless of whether f is continuous or convex. It
   reflects the general fact that min and max do not commute.
   This may be derived using the following pointwise inequality, which is true for all
   x ∈ X and y ∈ Y :

                                min f (x′ , y) ≤ f (x, y) ≤ max f (x, y ′ ).
                                x′ ∈X                        ′
                                                             y ∈Y

   Taking maxy on the left and minx on the right yields the desired minimax inequality:

                             max min     f (x, y)    ≤    min max      f (x, y).
                             y∈Y x∈X                      x∈X y∈Y

   This is often discussed in the context of zero-sum game theory. In zero-sum games,
   f (x, y) represents the payoff function for Player A choosing x and Player B choosing
   y. Then minx maxy f (x, y) is the best worst-case outcome Player A can guarantee,
   while maxy minx f (x, y) is the best guaranteed outcome Player B can ensure. The
   minimax inequality reflects that, in general, the first player is at a disadvantage,
   since they commit to a strategy without knowledge of the other’s move.

Geometry of Lagrange Multipliers

Geometrically, for x∗ to be an optimal solution, the gradient of f (x∗ ) can have no compo-
nent tangent to h(x) = 0 at x∗ . A tangent direction d to h(x) = 0 satisfies ∇h(x) · d = 0.
These tangent directions are feasible directions in which we can move the variable x to
improve the objective function while still maintaining the constraint equation. If ∇f im-
proves in one of these feasible tangent directions, then the current point is not optimal.
Thus, ∇f must be normal to h(x), meaning that it is in the span of the normals ∇hj (x).
    This is simple to visualize for a single constraint h(x) = 0 in two-dimensions, x ∈ R2 ,
as in Fig. 6.1. If f (x) decreases in a direction that is tangent to h, meaning that ∇f (x)
has a component tangent to h, then we can achieve a solution that is closer to optimal
by moving in this direction. Thus, a necessary condition for optimality is that ∇f (x) is
orthogonal to the tangent space at h, which means it is in the direction of ∇h. We will
revisit and generalize this geometric intuition to higher dimensions and more constraints
soon.

      The conditions for optimality of a solution (x∗ , λ∗ ) are the following:

                                             ∇x L(x∗ , λ∗ ) = 0,                                       (6.4a)
                                                     ∗   ∗
                                             ∇λ L(x , λ ) = 0.                                        (6.4b)

These are the first-order optimality conditions or the stationarity conditions, and they are a
subset of the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions, which will be discussed in Sec. 6.2.
   The second condition amounts to the constraint being enforced at (x∗ , λ∗ ), as

                                 ∇λ L(x∗ , λ∗ ) = 0      =⇒     h(x∗ ) = 0.                                (6.5)

The first condition yields the following expression

                      ∇x L(x∗ , λ∗ ) = 0     =⇒       ∇x f (x∗ ) + λ∗T ∇x h(x∗ ) = 0.                      (6.6)

This implies the following

                                       ∇x f (x∗ ) = −λ∗T ∇x h(x∗ ).                                        (6.7)

For a single equality constraint h(x) = 0, this simplifies to

                                        ∇x f (x∗ ) = −λ∗ ∇x h(x∗ ),                                        (6.8)

which is the same as the geometric interpretation that ∇f must have no component in the
tangent direction to h(x) = 0 at an optimal x∗ . Said another way, ∇f must point in a
direction normal to the constraint surface defined by h(x) = 0, given by ∇h. If this is not
true, then we can locally improve the objective by moving in a feasible direction.
    In this example, feasible directions d at a point x satisfy

                                              ∇h(x) · d = 0.

Optimality of x∗ requires that the directional derivative of the objective function vanishes
in every feasible direction d:

                              ∇f (x∗ ) · d = 0 ∀ d with ∇h(x∗ ) · d = 0.

Hence ∇f (x∗ ) must be orthogonal to the tangent space and therefore lies in the one-dimensional
normal space spanned by ∇h(x∗ ):

                                           ∇f (x∗ ) = −λ∇h(x∗ )

The scalar λ is the Lagrange multiplier, providing the exact rescaling of the normals.
    When we have more constraint equations, we will see that there may be several normal
directions to the constraint equations, and the gradient of the objective function must be
in this constraint-normal subspace. The values of these constraint normals that add up to
equal −∇f are the Lagrange multipliers in higher dimensions.

Simple Example

solve a constrained optimization problem. Consider f (x) to be a linear objective function
cT x = x + 2y with the constraint that our solution remains on the unit circle x2 + y 2 = 1:

                                             min x + 2y
                                             x,y

                                             s.t. x2 + y 2 = 1.

Convexity and Sufficient Conditions
The optimality conditions above are necessary conditions for a state x∗ to be optimal. In the
case of a convex problem, then they are also sufficient. Again, for the problem to be convex,
h(x) = 0 must be an affine constraint Ax = b. However, if either f or the feasible set
defined by h(x) = 0 are nonconvex, then the optimality conditions may not be sufficient.
Essentially, these conditions just indicate a stationary point, but it may be a local minimum,
maximum, or saddle point. We must then introduce second-order conditions to test for
optimality. Again, we let Tx be the tangent space to the constraint manifold at a point x:

                                       Tx = {d | ∇h(x) · d = 0} .

as long as d ̸= 0. Figure 6.4 shows an example of a nonconvex constraint equation that

Regularity Conditions
The use of Lagrange multipliers requires a mild set of regularity conditions, called con-
straint qualifications, to ensure the KKT conditions hold at a solution. These conditions
rule out pathological cases, such as when the active constraints at a point are not linearly
independent or when the feasible region has no interior.
    For equality-constrained problems, a key regularity condition is that gradients of the
active constraints must be linearly independent at x∗ , so the Jacobian ∇h(x∗ ) has full rank:
                                                           
                            rank ∇h1 (x∗ ) . . . ∇hm (x∗ ) = m.

This ensures that the set of feasible directions (i.e., the tangent space to the constraint sur-
face) has the expected dimension. If the constraint gradients are linearly dependent, then
instead of intersecting transversely to form a well-behaved surface, the constraints may
define a degenerate feasible region. Thus, there may not be Lagrange multipliers that sat-
isfy the stationarity condition, even at a point that minimizes the objective and satisfies the
constraints. For example, two equality constraints in 2D whose gradients point in the same
direction define parallel lines so the feasible region may be empty or ill-defined. Similar
regularity conditions exist for the more general KKT theory of inequality constraints.

History
Lagrange first introduced the method of multipliers to eliminate constraint forces in holo-
nomic3 mechanics [294, 332]. Figure 6.5 shows a simple mechanical system of a block slid-
ing down an inclined plane, where the plane exerts a force to keep the block exactly on the
boundary; this force is a Lagrange multiplier enforcing the constraint that the block remains
exactly on the boundary of the inclined plane. We will discuss the broader connections of
constraints, Lagrange multipliers, and classical mechanics in Section 6.6.
    Nearly two hundred years later, this approach was generalized to handle inequality
constraints by Karush [270] and popularized by Kuhn and Tucker [290], marking the birth
of modern nonlinear programming. Since then, the field has developed rapidly, with the
theory of duality and the primal-dual algorithms that followed.
   3
    A holonomic constraint is a constraint on a mechanical system that can be expressed as an explicit equation
relating the generalized coordinates, typically in the form h(q1 , q2 , . . . , qn , t) = 0. Such constraints restrict
the configuration space but not the velocities independently. In contrast, nonholonomic constraints involve
the velocities and cannot be integrated to purely positional constraints. For example, a coin rolling without
slipping is a non-holonomic system, while a block sliding down an incline is a holonomic system.

Algorithmic Perspectives
The KKT system of equations underpin many of the most powerful modern algorithms for
constrained optimization, including active-set, interior-point, and augmented Lagrangian
methods. These algorithms are closely related to the notion of duality, and we will discuss
them more in this Chapter and in Chapter 5. We provide a brief overview here:

  (a) Active-Set Methods (ASM) and Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP). Active
      set methods involve guessing which inequality constraints are active and become
      equality constraints. Then the unconstrained Lagrangian in Eq. (6.2) is solved, for
      example with Newton’s method. Finally, constraints are tested to see if they enter or
      exit after this iteration. In sequential quadratic programming, we approximate f (x)
      with a quadratic.

 (b) Interior-Point Methods (IPM). IPMs attempt to iteratively solve for an optimal point
     (x∗ , λ∗ , µ∗ ) that satisfy the KKT conditions. We replace each gj (x) ≤ 0 in the La-
     grangian objective function with a log-barrier function −t log(−gj (x)). A Newton it-
     eration on the barrier-augmented objective function will enforce gj (x) ≤ 0. As t → 0,
     the solution converges to a KKT point. We will explore IPMs in detail in Section 6.4.

  (c) Augmented Lagrangian Methods (ALM). Here we rewrite our inequality constraints
      g(x) ≤ 0 as equality constraints by introducing slack variables: g(x)+s = 0 with s ≥
      0. We then write the augmented Lagrangian to include penalty terms ρ2 ∥g(x) + s∥2 to

Regularity Conditions
There are regularity conditions, known as constraint qualifications, for when a constrained
optimizer x∗ will satisfy the KKT conditions. The regularity conditions are more compli-
cated in the case of mixed equality and inequality constraints than those for simple equality
constraints. In this case, several constraint qualifications can be used:
   • Slater’s Condition. For convex problems, Slater’s condition is a powerful and prac-
     tical regularity test. It simply requires the existence of a strictly feasible point x0 :
                                      h(x0 ) = 0     and g(x0 ) < 0
     so that all inequality and equality constraints are satisfied strictly at x0 . This must
     only be true at a single feasible x0 , and gives a global result. Slater’s condition guar-
     antees strong duality and ensures the KKT conditions are necessary and sufficient for
     optimality in convex problems. For linear programs, Slater’s condition automatically
     holds if the feasible set has a non-empty interior. For nonconvex problems, Slater’s
     condition does not guarantee strong duality or the sufficiency of the KKT conditions.
     It may still ensure that the KKT conditions are necessary for optimality, but only if
     other constraint qualifications, such as LICQ or MFCQ, are also satisfied.
   • Linear Independence Constraint Qualification (LICQ). This is one of the most com-
     monly used regularity conditions. As before, the gradients of all active constraints
     (i.e., equality constraints h(x) = 0 and inequality constraints gj (x∗ ) = 0 active at x∗ )
     must be linearly independent. LICQ ensures that if x∗ is a local minimizer, then there
     exist unique Lagrange multipliers (λ, µ) that satisfy the KKT conditions. LICQ alone
     does not ensure existence of an optimizer or sufficiency of KKT. In the convex case,
     LICQ is stronger than Slater for existence and isn’t needed for sufficiency.
   • Mangasarian–Fromovitz Constraint Qualification (MFCQ). This is a bit weaker than
     LICQ and states that the equality constraint gradients ∇h(x∗ ) are linearly indepen-
     dent, and that there exists a direction d ∈ Rn such that:
                  ∇hi (x∗ ) · d = 0 for all i,        ∇gj (x∗ ) · d < 0 for all active j.
     This guarantees the existence of a feasible direction that moves away from the active
     inequality constraints. Unlike LICQ, MFCQ allows for linear dependence among
     active inequalities, as long as there is a direction of descent that maintains feasibility.

6.3      Duality
Duality is one of the most profound and useful concepts in optimization. For every con-
strained optimization problem, there is a dual optimization problem that provides a lower
bound to the optimum of the original primal problem. We have already seen a simple ex-
ample of a dual optimization problem in Chapter 3 on linear programming. These ideas
generalize naturally to other convex and nonconvex optimization problems. Regardless of
the convexity of the primal problem, the dual problem is always concave.
    The dual optimization problem is closely related to Lagrange multipliers and the KKT
conditions. We will show that the Lagrange multipliers λ and µ are actually decision vari-
ables for the dual problem. When only equality constraints are present, this dual problem
is unconstrained. For a mix of equality and inequality constraints, the dual has positivity
constraints on the Lagrange multipliers µ corresponding to the inequality constraints.
    Primal–dual interior-point methods are a class of algorithms for modern high-dimensional
constrained optimization, discussed in Section 6.4 Duality is also important in the alter-
nating direction method of multipliers (ADMM), discussed in Section 6.5, and branch-
and-bound algorithms for combinatorial and nonconvex problems. These approaches are
central for many problems we have already seen, such as box-constrained least squares,
LASSO, and ridge regression. When the dual equals the primal, then no further improve-
ment is possible, providing a certificate of optimality.

Geometric Interpretation of Duality
A key geometric insight behind Lagrange duality is the relationship between supporting
hyperplanes15 and the construction of the dual function. In convex optimization, duality
is not just an algebraic formula, but it is also a geometric process of constructing the best
possible linear lower bound on a convex function, subject to constraints.
    For simplicity, we consider equality constraints h(x) = 0. In the primal, we search for
the lowest level set of f that intersects the feasible region defined by h(x) = 0. In the dual,
we search over λ for the best affine lower bound (i.e., supporting hyperplane) on f (x) that
respects the constraint. The optimal λ∗ is the slope of this best supporting hyperplane.

6.4     Interior Point Methods
Interior point methods17 (IPMs) are a family of iterative algorithms to solve constrained
optimization problems by traversing the interior of the feasible region rather than explor-
ing the boundary [65, 366]. We’ve seen the simplex method to solve linear programs in
Chapter 3, which moves from boundary vertex to vertex until an optimal solution is found.
IPMs are an alternative approach that scale to very high-dimensional problems with com-
plex boundaries and generalize to more sophisticated convex and nonconvex problems.
    The interior point method finds solutions that satisfy the KKT conditions, and hence
are optimal. For convex problems this optimality is global, with polynomial-time com-
plexity. The basic principle of IPMs is to replace the original constrained optimization
problem with a series of unconstrained subproblems. A common example is the barrier
approach, which enforces strict interior feasibility by introducing a barrier, or penalty, that
becomes infinite at the boundary of the feasible region. This is the approach we take here.
Specifically, we present a primal approach18 that utilizes a logarithmic barrier function.
    It is often difficult to solve the KKT conditions in Eq. (6.16) directly, so instead we solve
a family of simpler related problems that converge to the KKT solution. We explain this for
a single constraint g(x) ≤ 0, though it generalizes to more constraints. The first three KKT
conditions stay the same, and the last condition changes from µg(x) = 0 to µg(x) = −t,
where t is an auxiliary variable that we slowly relax to t = 0, in which case we recover
the original KKT system. It is simpler to solve these perturbed equations for x∗ (t), and we
may then vary t to 0, using the solution x∗ (tk−1 ) as the initial condition to solve for x∗ (tk ).
In the limit, we satisfy the KKT conditions and the inequality constraint. This is known as
continuation, or homotopy, and it is a very useful technique beyond optimization.

A solution to this problem will satisfy the first KKT condition and will also ensure that
g(x) ≤ 0, since −g(x) must be positive for log(−g(x)) to be finite. Similarly, from Eq. (6.49),
µ ≥ 0. Thus, all modified KKT conditions will be satisfied. The function −t log(−g(x)) is
known as a barrier function, since it is a smooth convex function, defined in the interior
of the feasible set, that becomes very large near the boundaries. As t → 0, this barrier
becomes infinite, as shown in Fig. 6.12 for two inequality constraints.
     Since this is an unconstrained optimization problem, we may use simpler techniques,
such as Newton’s method, which converges rapidly with a good initial condition. When t
is small, the penalty function t log(−g(x)) will be very steep at the boundaries, and shallow
elsewhere, so Newton’s method may converge slowly. However, using the optimal solu-
tion x∗ (tk ) as the initial condition for Newton’s method at tk+1 accelerates the algorithm.
     If we start with a large t, the objective function appears to have no f (x) term, and in-
stead we minimize − log(−g(x)), which is equivalent to minimizing g(x). We can solve this
using Newton’s method to find x∗ (∞), called the analytic center of the feasible set, which
will be an initialization for our first value of t. The sequence of intermediate solutions
x∗ (tk ) is called the central path, and it is always in the interior of the feasible set.

6.5     Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM)
The alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) [64, 376] is a splitting method.
It allows us to solve convex optimization problems by decomposing them into smaller,
more manageable sub-problems, especially those that can be written as the sum of two
functions that are individually easier to minimize. It combines the decomposability of
dual ascent methods with the robustness of the method of multipliers. ADMM is widely
used in applications, including signal processing, machine learning, and control. Several
specific applications include robust PCA [90], MAP inference [336, 345], LP decoding [26],
graphical models [122], and sparse feedback control [308]. We will explore robust PCA in
the next subsection. ADMM is related to earlier operator splitting methods and penalty-
duality schemes [151, 194, 182, 313, 160]. Because of its widespread applicability, ADMM
has inspired many efficient software implementations [64, 370]. There are also a number of
works to develop theoretical results concerning guarantees and rates of convergence [221,
197, 161].
    There are two common formulations, the consensus form and the composite form.
These are equivalent, and will both be discussed here.

Consensus form of ADMM. A standard form of ADMM may be written as

                                          min f (x) + g(z)
                                           x,z

                                           s.t. Ax + Bz = c.

This form of ADMM is suitable for splitting across x and z. It allows for distributed com-
puting when f and g are separable and makes use of the augmented Lagrangian:
                                                              ρ
              Lρ (x, z, λ) = f (x) + g(z) + λT (Ax + Bz − c) + ∥Ax + Bz − c∥2 .
                                                              2

Composite form of ADMM. A special form of consensus ADMM may be written as

                                           min f (x) + g(Ax).
                                            x

using a variable substitution:
                                                 z = Ax.
Then the problem becomes:

                                min    f (x) + g(z)     s.t. Ax − z = 0.
                                x,z

This matches the consensus form with:

                                            B = −I,      c = 0.

The two forms are equivalent; the second is a more structured special case that is easier to
apply to problems like LASSO, total variation denoising, or compressed sensing.

    We introduce the new variables z = Ax to decouple the problem, making it less stiff
and more easily distributed. If g is nonsmooth or difficult to minimize in composition, then
minimizing f (x) + g(Ax) directly might be hard. Introducing z allows one to alternatively
minimize f (x) with a quadratic penalty, then minimize g(z) separately, for example us-
ing known proximal operators; this process is repeated until convergence. The constraint
Ax = z is then enforced softly via the dual variable and the quadratic penalty.


Primal-Dual Interpretation. ADMM is fundamentally a primal-dual algorithm. ADMM
is a special case of the augmented Lagrangian method, which itself is a primal-dual method.
In this more general approach, we form the augmented Lagrangian with respect to the con-
straint (e.g., Ax + Bz = c), and then alternate the updates, first minimizing with respect
to the primal variables (x, z), and then performing a gradient ascent on the dual variable
(λ). This differs from standard Lagrangian dual ascent, which may suffer from slow con-
vergence or instability. ADMM can be viewed as a dual ascent method with proximal
regularization, which dampens the dual updates with a penalty to encourage stability and
convergence. This is an application of the Douglas-Rachford algorithm [151] to the dual
problem [279].

6.6     Calculus of Variations and the Euler-Lagrange Equations
The calculus of variations is a field of mathematical analysis that deals with maximiz-
ing or minimizing functionals, which are mappings from a space of functions to the real
numbers [186, 163]. For example, when optimizing the shape of a wing to maximize lift
and minimize drag, the shape may be represented as a function to be optimized, given
some constraints (structural, size, weight, etc.). In contrast to conventional optimization,
where the unknowns are finite-dimensional vectors, variational problems involve opti-
mizing over infinite-dimensional spaces of functions. This broader setting underlies many
topics, including optimal control, PDE-constrained optimization, and machine learning.
    The calculus of variations dates back to Johann Bernoulli’s brachistochrone problem in the
late 17th century. Euler and Lagrange developed the field in the 18th century, including
the Euler-Lagrange equation and functional derivatives. Over time, the field evolved to
include more general functional spaces, such as Sobolev and Banach spaces.
    Today, variational calculus is vital in many areas:
   • Physics and Mechanics: Action principles [1], based on variational principles, are
     central in classical mechanics, general relativity, and quantum field theory.
   • PDE-Constrained Optimization (Chapter 8): Many engineering problems involve
     optimizing a geometric shape given the constraints of some physics, typically ex-
     pressed as a partial differential equation. For example, designing the shape of cool-
     ing fins in a heat exchanger [297], or the shape of a wing on a modern commercial
     aircraft [247] are both constrained variational optimization problems.
   • Optimal Control (Chapter 9): Most optimal control problems involve solving for a
     function to minimize or maximize a given objective function, constrained by dynam-
     ics. The Pontryagin maximum principle, the Kalman filter, and dynamic program-
     ming are three concrete examples that have roots in variational arguments [59, 468,
     37].
   • Machine Learning (Chapter 10): Many problems in machine learning may be viewed
     through a variational lens [546, 249, 275]. Deep neural networks often approximate
     functionals, such as deep operator networks [320] and neural operators [307, 306].

systems, although they provide a much more general set of optimality conditions for vari-

Relationship to Fenchel Conjugate. The Legendre transform applies to smooth, convex

(e.g., energy vs. entropy, Lagrangian vs. Hamiltonian).

but the Legendre transform assumes differentiability and full-domain convexity. In partic-

One can state the infinite-dimensional optimization problem as:

                               minimize J [y]
                               subject to y ∈ Y,
                                             gj [y] ≥ 0,    j = 1, . . . , m,

Lagrange; primal feasibility, meaning that the constraints are satisfied; dual feasibility,
meaning that the Lagrange multipliers µj ≥ 0; complementary slackness, µj gj [y] = 0 for
each i; and possibly boundary or transversality conditions. These conditions are infinite-
dimensional analogs of those in finite-dimensional convex optimization [229].
    In indirect methods, one formally applies the Euler-Lagrange equation, or KKT condi-
tions, to obtain differential equations and solves them analytically or numerically. In con-
trast, direct methods discretize the function space, via finite elements or finite differences,
and then solve the resulting large, finite-dimensional problem using standard optimization
tools such as interior point methods or sequential quadratic programming [366].

    A common workflow in PDE-constrained optimization involves representing y(x) by
a set of finite parameters (e.g., piecewise polynomials), approximating J [y] and any con-
straints by summations or quadrature, and solving the resulting finite-dimensional opti-
mization problem using gradient or Newton iterations. This approach is popular due to
existing solvers that can handle large-scale, sparse systems efficiently.
    Alternatively, one may solve the variational (KKT) system at the continuous level first
and then discretize. The advantage is a more precise expression of conditions such as
adjoint equations. However, it can be more complex to implement and analyze.


Constraints as Additional Conditions. In many mechanical systems, one has holonomic
constraints expressible as ϕ(q) = 0. Such a constraint can be incorporated into the La-
grangian through the method of Lagrange multipliers or by reduction [56]. When one sets
up L′ = L + λ ϕ(q), the variation with respect to λ yields ϕ(q) = 0, and variation with
respect to q yields the Euler–Lagrange equations with an additional λ ∂ϕ/∂q term.
    An alternative is to interpret constraints as restricting the space of admissible curves
q(t). The variation is then taken only among curves satisfying ϕ(q(t)) ≡ 0 for all t. The
final outcome is that the constraint is satisfied at each instant, and the Euler–Lagrange
equations incorporate the constraint forces, typically signaled by a Lagrange multiplier.
