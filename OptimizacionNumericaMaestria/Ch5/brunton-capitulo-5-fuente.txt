Chapter 5

Nonsmooth and Global Optimization

In this chapter, we explore several methods for nonsmooth (i.e., gradient-free) and non-
convex optimization. This chapter will have a bit less structure than the previous chapters,
because the topic of nonconvex optimization is itself less structured. This is a vast field,
and we will only cover some of the major highlights.
     First, we will develop the proximal gradient method, which is an incredibly useful
extension of gradient descent for nonsmooth problems where the gradient may not ex-
ist. Many optimization problems have a smooth objective, such as the least squares error
function, and a nonsmooth regularizer, such as the ℓ1 regularized LASSO regression from
Chapter 4. The proximal gradient approach is a powerful and intuitive algorithmic ap-
proach to handle these situations. The proximal operator, used in the proximal gradient
method, will also be useful in the next chapter, when we introduce the alternating di-
rection method of multipliers (ADMM). Proximal algorithms are essential when we have
nonsmooth terms in our objective function, such as ∥ · ∥1 , the nuclear norm ∥ · ∥∗ , the total
variation used in image processing in Chapter 8, and other nonsmooth regularizers used
in neural network training (see Chapter 10).
     Next, we will explore two workhorse algorithms that assume very little structure in
the optimization problem: the Nelder-Mead downhill simplex method and simulated an-
nealing. The downhill simplex method, not to be confused with Dantzig’s simplex method
from linear programming, uses a simplex (i.e., a polytope) to sample the objective function
landscape and then take steps that deform the simplex towards better objectives. Sim-
ulated annealing replicates the natural process of annealing, or cooling, when creating
strong metal alloys, where a specific cooling schedule is used to promote strong crys-
tal structures in the material. In simulated annealing, the optimization algorithm begins
“hot”, exploring the objective function landscape, and gradually “cools” to begin exploit-
ing favorable local minima.
     We then introduce evolutionary algorithms, including genetic algorithms and genetic
programming. Genetic programming is particularly interesting in the context of modern
explainable and interpretable AI.
     Finally, we will cover distributed and swarm optimization approaches, such as particle
swarm optimization and ant colony optimization. These approaches leverage distributed
optimization to mimic the collective behavior of natural biological systems, such as fish
schooling, birds flocking, and insects swarming.

5.1     Proximal Gradient Algorithms
Standard gradient descent from Chapter 2 no longer applies to optimization problem with
a nonsmooth regularizer, such as the ℓ1 norm in LASSO or total variation penalties, due
to the non-differentiability of the objective. The proximal gradient method extends gradient
descent to handle such composite optimization problems of the form

                                           min     f (x) + g(x),                                          (5.1)
                                            x


where f (x) is smooth, and g(x) is possibly nonsmooth. Typically, we assume additional
structure on f and g, such as f being convex with Lipschitz continuous gradient, and g
being convex. However, these are not strictly necessary for much of the discussion and
practical implementation. A Lipschitz continuous gradient of f means that we can bound
the function from above by a simple quadratic, which will be used in the derivation below.

Panel 5.1: The Convex Hegemony
   The centrality of convexity in optimization emerged in the mid-20th century. Af-
   ter Dantzig’s simplex method [123] made linear programming practical, researchers
   realized that convexity was the underlying property that guaranteed tractability.
   Local minima of convex problems are also global, and convex functions admit pow-
   erful duality theory. Rockafellar’s 1970 Convex Analysis [414] codified this view, giv-
   ing mathematicians and engineers a common language for convex sets, cones, and
   subgradients. In the 1970s and 1980s, convex quadratic programming and conic for-
   mulations became essential for operations research, control, and engineering design.
   Interior point methods, in particular, reinforced the idea that convex optimization
   was not only elegant but also scalable to real-world problems [268, 363, 536]. By the
   1990s, the convex hegemony was firmly established: researchers habitually reshaped
   models to fit convex templates because these were the only problems they could
   solve reliably at scale.
   In the last two decades, this dominance has softened. The incredible growth of com-
   puting power and available data, together with algorithmic advances in splitting
   and proximal methods, showed that many nonconvex problems could be attacked
   directly. Forward–backward splitting and proximal gradient methods [110, 109],
   ADMM [64], and more general convergence frameworks [16, 58] demonstrated
   practical ways to handle structured nonconvex problems. Meanwhile, the suc-
   cess of deep learning [203] made nonconvexity mainstream: billions of parameters
   trained by first-order methods challenged the old intuition that nonconvex meant
   intractable. Today convex optimization is still foundational [65], guiding algorithms
   and serving as a benchmark. However, the field now is less dogmatic, with non-
   convex approaches now seen as legitimate, effective tools rather than mathematical
   dead ends.

Constrained Optimization and the Projected Gradient Algorithm
It is possible to handle constraints with the proximal gradient, by adding them directly
into the objective function, via an infinite penalty for x not in a feasible set C:
                                                
                                                    0, x ∈ C
                               g(x) = δC (x) =
                                                   ∞, x ∈  / C.

This function is nonsmooth, discontinuous, and unbounded. However, we can still com-
pute the proximal gradient of this function, which will allow us to develop a simple
gradient-based descent algorithm for constrained optimization problems.
   Specifically, we may write the proximal gradient operator out explicitly

                               prox 1       = argminx β2 ∥x − z∥22 + δC (x)
                                    β ,δC

                                            = argminx∈C β2 ∥x − z∥22
                                            ≡ projC (z).

The proximal gradient operator takes a gradient step on f (x) and then projects onto the
feasible set C. This projection operator may or may not be simple to compute. For example,
if C is a sphere or is a box, then it is simple to project z onto this set.
     The basic idea of the projected gradient algorithm is to start with an initial x0 in the
feasible set, take an unconstrained gradient iteration step, and project the solution back
into the feasible set. We then iterate until convergence.

Splitting Methods
The proximal operator, in the proximal gradient method above, generalizes to a broad
family of operator splitting methods. In splitting methods, one decomposes a problem
with multiple terms in the objective function, introducing extra variables to handle each
term separately. These separate variables are then coupled with an additional term in
the objective. This has several benefits that we will explore in this section via the sparse,
relaxed, regularized regression (SR3) method, and via ADMM2 in Section 6.5.
    The general idea behind splitting methods is to transform a problem of the form

                                             min    f (x) + g(x)                                           (5.7)
                                              x

into the form
                                                          ρ
                                    min     f (x) + g(z) + ∥x − z∥2 .                                      (5.8)
                                     x,z                  2
Again, the regularization term g(x) could be used to promote specific properties, such as
sparsity, or enforce constraints, as discussed in Chapters 4 and 6. These terms each have
different decision variables x and z, which are coupled through the quadratic ρ2 ∥x − z∥2 .
The parameter ρ determine the tightness of this coupling. Technically, Eq. (5.8) solves a dif-
ferent problem that approximates Eq. (5.7); to exactly solve Eq. (5.7), we add the constraint
x = z via Lagrange multipliers, which is the approach taken in ADMM.
    This approach has several benefits. We break a complex problem into simpler sub-
problems, which are each better conditioned.
    We may also use different algorithms for each term, for example using a precondi-
tioned least squares solver for the f (x), while using a proximal gradient descent algorithm
for the nonsmooth g(x).
    Many problems can be written in this form. For example, the least-squares regression
problem with objective function f (x) = 12 ∥Ax − b∥2 can be written

Sparse, Relaxed, Regularized Regression (SR3)
Sparse, relaxed, regularized regression (SR3) [551] is a powerful framework for handling
nonsmooth and nonconvex sparse optimization problems in a fast and accurate way. Real-
world optimization problems are often ill-posed or numerically unstable. Regularization
and relaxation are two common strategies to mitigate these challenges. Regularization mod-
ifies an objective function to improve stability, interpretability, or generalization. Relaxation
replaces nonconvex terms in the constraints or the objective function with convex approx-
imations that are amenable to computations. When combined, regularization and relax-
ation provide a powerful framework to systematically handle large-scale, ill-conditioned,
or combinatorially complex problems. Splitting methods are effective ways of solving
regularized, relaxed optimization problems. Instead of satisfying all constraints and objec-
tives with the same decision variable, splitting introduces new decision variables for each
term, adding a final regularization term to tightly couple these independent optimization
problems. This makes stiff optimization problems more tractable. For convex problems,
the SR3 formulation is part of a larger class of problems [30], where it is possible to use
alternating minimization algorithms [2]. The original alternating projection methods go
back to the 1950s by Von Neumann [516] and Cheney and Goldstein [103].
     Sparse optimization is ubiquitous, and SR3 splits the sparsity and accuracy require-
ments in Eq. (5.9) between the z and x variables. The algorithm iterates between solving
the subproblems in x and z individually, combining ρ2 ∥x − z∥22 and g(z) in a proximal gra-
dient step. The x term can be solved for using preconditioned least squares. It is possible
to partially minimize x to obtain a function entirely in z, which has been shown to have
nearly spherical level sets, shown schematically in Fig. 5.3 in Zheng et al. [551]. This is in
contrast to the highly eccentric elliptical level sets of ∥Ax − b∥2 .
     These concepts will be used in the later chapters on inverse problems3 , control theory,
and machine learning, where relaxation and regularization are essential. Machine learning
examples include sparse regression (LASSO), ridge regression, robust fitting, and training
neural networks under constraints, such as derived from physics. In signal processing
and image analysis, we use total variation (TV) regularization to denoise, wavelet-based
sparsity constraints and sparse regularization for image reconstruction given sparse mea-
surements, and nuclear norm regularization for matrix completion. We have already seen
examples of relaxing integer constraints to a continuous linear program. Finally, in ro-
bust control and PDE-constrained optimization, small changes in model parameters can
produce unstable or ill-posed results, necessitating regularization and relaxation.
     As a concrete example of the improvement using SR3, we consider the LASSO prob-
lem, investigated in Zheng et al. [551]. It was shown in Su et al. [472] that when perform-
ing LASSO, false discoveries occur early in the optimization path that cannot be reversed,
leading to many false positives compared to true positives. This is largely attributed to
the eccentricity of the geometry of the optimization problem, which is largely mitigated

5.2     Nelder-Mead Downhill Simplex
The Nelder-Mead Downhill Simplex method is a popular gradient-free, local search tech-
nique for unconstrained optimization problems. It was introduced by John Nelder and
Roger Mead in 1965 [359]. Unlike gradient-based methods, which require information
about the gradient or Hessian of an objective function, Nelder-Mead works purely with
function value evaluations. It is particularly useful when the objective function is non-
differentiable or discontinuous in certain regions, or when numerical or analytical gra-
dients are difficult or expensive to obtain. Typically this approach works well when the
dimension of the problem is relatively small, commonly up to a few dozen variables.
    Despite its popularity in practice and its ease of implementation, the Nelder–Mead
method does not have strong theoretical convergence guarantees. In fact, there are known
counterexamples, even for strictly convex functions [343], where the method fails to con-
verge or its performance degrades. Nevertheless, it remains widely used in engineering
and applied mathematics for small to medium-scale problems [470], particularly when
function evaluations are expensive and derivative approximations or adjoint-based ap-
proaches are complicated or infeasible.
    The idea behind the downhill simplex method is quite simple. Consider a minimiza-
tion problem. Given a decision variable x ∈ Rn , we initialize a simplex in Rn with n + 1
vertices {xj }nj=0 , evaluate the objective function at all vertices, and modify the vertex with
the highest objective function value to ideally move to a lower objective function value.
In this way, we iteratively move the centroid of the simplex to a lower objective function
value, one vertex at a time, without needing to compute a gradient at any step. In a sense,
this simplex “rolls down” the objective function landscape; see Fig. 5.5 for an illustration.
There are several details and options for how to modify the vertex with the highest value,
depending on the dimension of the problem, the structure of the objective function land-
scape, among other considerations. In addition, we may shrink or expand the size of the
simplex depending on properties of the objective function landscape.
    This may be mathematically formalized as follows. At each step, the current simplex
is defined by the points {x0 , x1 , · · · , xn }. We assume that the points are sorted so that

where f is the objective function being minimized. We define the centroid of the n best

portant to reiterate the strengths and limitations of this approach. The downhill simplex
method is very simple to implement and requires no gradient or higher-order derivative
information, making it a reasonable choice for many situations. It is also possible to code
up from scratch and implement special or custom features. Thus, the algorithm is often
effective for small-scale problems where function evaluations are costly, or gradient ap-
proximations are infeasible.
    However, there are several drawbacks as well. There are relatively few convergence
results for nonconvex problems, and convergence to a local minima is not guaranteed to be
global. However, the algorithm can often navigate several nearby local minima depending
on the size of the initial simplex and optimization hyperparameters. In higher dimensions,
the downhill simplex algorithm may perform poorly, often requiring many function eval-
uations. Finally, without careful tuning, the method might stagnate or roam slowly around
an optimum.
    However, in practice, the Nelder-Mead downhill simplex method remains one of the
most widely used derivative-free local optimization techniques [113]. Its simple concep-
tual framework and relatively straightforward hyperparameter setup make it attractive
for problems in low to moderate dimensions, particularly when function evaluations are
expensive or noisy but one still prefers a local method to refine near a suspected optimum.

5.3     Simulated Annealing
Simulated annealing is a stochastic global optimization technique inspired by the met-
allurgical process of annealing, in which a material is heated and then slowly cooled to
decrease defects and obtain a strong crystalline structure [277, 515]; see panel Panel 5.3. In
metallurgical annealing, the specific cooling schedule makes a tremendous difference in
the final properties of the metal. In optimization, simulated annealing uses a similar idea,
leveraging an initially higher “temperature” to explore the solution space and escape local
minima, then gradually lowering the temperature to focus on refining solutions near good
local minima. In simulated annealing, the temperature refers to the step size of random
perturbations that are made to the current solution at each iteration step, so that a higher
temperature leads to larger, more exploratory, random fluctuations.
    Simulated annealing is a robust, global search algorithm that is suitable for nonconvex
optimization problems. Moreover, it does not require gradient information and is able

We aim to minimize an objective function f (x). The decision variable x may be continuous
(i.e., x ∈ Rn ) or discrete. Simulated annealing iteratively samples a new candidate solution
xk+1 in the neighborhood of the current solution xk . The difference in objective values
∆ = f (xk+1 ) − f (xk ) guides the acceptance of xk+1 :

  1. If ∆ ≤ 0 then the new solution is better, and we accept it unconditionally.
                                                                                        
  2. If ∆ > 0, the new solution is worse, and we accept it with probability p = exp − ∆
                                                                                      T ,
     where T is the current temperature.

     Over time, T is decreased according to the cooling schedule, reducing the acceptance of
worse solutions. The algorithm then focuses more on local refinement near good solutions.
Typical stopping criteria include a minimum temperature threshold, a maximum number
of iterations, or detecting convergence.

Cooling Schedules, Hyperparameters, and Tuning. There are several choices for the
cooling schedule. A classical geometric decay for temperature is
                                              Tk+1 = α Tk ,
where 0 < α < 1 is a constant (e.g., 0.9 ≤ α ≤ 0.99). More sophisticated schedules adjust
temperature dynamically based on recent acceptance rates.

   There are also some theoretical guarantees for cooling rates Tk that lie between the
curves 1/k and 1/k 2 . In addition, there are several hyperparameters that must be tuned:

   • Initial temperature T0 : T0 should be high enough to allow frequent acceptance of
     worse moves at the start to encourage global exploration. Too large of a T0 will
     increase the number of iterations required before the temperature is low enough for
     local exploitation of a local minima.

   • Cooling factor α: For geometric decay, this determines how fast T is reduced in each
     iteration. Too fast, and the algorithm won’t have time to explore, and too slow and
     the algorithm may not converge quickly or to a good minima.

   • Neighborhood function or “move operator”: This determines how new candidate
     solutions are generated from the current solution. In continuous domains, one might
     add small random Gaussian or uniform perturbations. In discrete domains (e.g.,
     traveling salesman), it might be a random permutation swap.

   • Stopping criteria: Several choices include reaching a minimum temperature Tmin , a
     maximum number of iterations, or no improvement for a number of steps.

Code Example
Here we explore the objective function in Eq. (5.10) with simulated annealing. Figure 5.11
shows the progression of the decision variable, starting from a “hot” exploratory condition
in red to the final “cold” nearly optimal condition in blue. It is simple to implement a
basic simulated annealing code. The following code tracks the best objective value at each
iteration and returns the path of visited points.
   def simulated_annealing(init_point, n_iter=200, T0=5.0, alpha=0.98,
      step_size=1.0, bounds=(-5,5)):
       current = np.array(init_point, dtype=float)
       current_val = f(current)
       path = [current.copy()]
       temp = T0 # Initial temperature

        for i in range(n_iter):
            # Propose random step
            candidate = current + np.random.randn(2) * step_size
            candidate = np.clip(candidate, bounds[0], bounds[1])
            candidate_val = f(candidate)
            delta = candidate_val - current_val

              # Accept if better or with probability eˆ(-delta/T)
              if delta < 0:
                  current, current_val = candidate, candidate_val
              else:
                  if np.random.rand() < np.exp(-delta / temp):
                      current, current_val = candidate, candidate_val

              temp *= alpha # Lower the temperature

5.4     Evolutionary Algorithms
Evolutionary algorithms form a broad class of methods for nonconvex optimization, based
on the principles of biological evolution and survival of the fittest. Evolutionary oper-
ations, such as replication, mutation, and exchange of genetic information, are used to
iteratively evolve populations of candidate solutions. With a judicious choice of hyperpa-
rameters, the performance distribution of this population will improve and individuals in
the population may approach globally optimal solutions. Evolutionary algorithms broadly
comprise two major categories of methods: genetic algorithms for parameter optimization
and genetic programming for function optimization.
    Parameter optimization is a simpler problem, where we learn the parameters of a func-
tion with known structure. The goal is to find the optimal parameters given some objective
function, for example training a neural network fitting ŷ = fθ (x) to fit data y, or trying to
learn the parameters of a control law with optimal performance.
    Function optimization is more challenging, because we are learning both the structure
and parameters of a function that optimizes a given objective function. Again, the task
may be to fit data with a learned approximation f (x) or learn a control law u = f (x).
However, in this case, we will also learn the structure of the function f .
    We begin with genetic algorithms, because of their simplicity and broad applicability,
and then proceed to the more challenging, but more flexible, class of genetic programming.
Genetic programming is often referred to as symbolic regression, because it yields analytic
representations of the function being optimized. This is useful for explainable AI [116].

Genetic algorithms
Genetic algorithms (GAs) are used for parameter optimization [234, 127, 195]. In GA, a
population of many realizations with different parameter values compete to minimize
a cost function, and successful parameter values are propagated to future generations
through a set of genetic rules. The parameters of a system are generally represented by
a binary sequence, as in Fig. 5.14. For example, these parameters may represent the three
control gains KP , KI , and KD for a proportional-integral-derivative (PID) controller [76].
A number of realizations with different parameter values, called individuals, are initial-
ized in a population and their performance is evaluated and compared on a well-defined
task. Successful individuals with a lower cost have a higher probability of being selected
to advance to the next generation, according to the following genetic operations:

       Replication: An individual is selected to advance to the next generation.

       Crossover: Two individuals are selected to exchange a portion of their code and ad-
       vance to the next generation; crossover exploits and enhances successful strategies.

       Mutation: An individual is selected to have some of its code modified with new val-
       ues; mutation promotes diversity and increases the exploration of parameter space.

       Elitism (optional): A set number of the most fit individuals are advanced directly to
       the next generation. Elitism is closely related to replication, but it involves a fixed
       number of the best individuals, rather than a random selection.

Genetic programming

Genetic programming (GP) [285, 284] is a powerful generalization of genetic algorithms
that simultaneously optimizes both the structure and parameters of an input–output map
(i.e., a function), as shown in Fig. 5.16. More recently, genetic programming has been used
extensively to learn dynamical systems [60, 442, 116] or control laws [158].
     The function tree representation in GP is quite flexible, enabling the encoding of com-
plex functions of the input data x through a recursive tree structure. Each branch is a
signal, and the merging points are mathematical operations. Inputs and constants are the
leaves, and the overall function output f (x) is the root. The genetic operations of crossover,
mutation, and replication are shown schematically in Fig. 5.17. Each of these operations
has a corresponding probability, p(C), p(M ), and p(R).
     In general, although evolutionary algorithms are extremely general and powerful,
there are very few theoretical guarantees on convergence and stability. That said, they
have had a resurgence of interest recently as a technique for learning human-interpretable
and explainable representations of machine learning models [119].

5.5     Distributed and Swarm Optimization
With the rise of big data, parallel computing, and interconnected systems, optimization
strategies that leverage multiple agents to solve sub-problems in a distributed manner
have become essential. Here we discuss two main paradigms: distributed optimization
and swarm optimization. Distributed optimization aims to solve large-scale problems by
decomposing them into subproblems that run in parallel, synchronizing partial solutions
through communication protocols [64, 357]. In contrast, swarm optimization takes inspira-
tion from natural phenomena, such as birds flocking, fish schooling, and ants foraging,
to design optimization algorithms. Particle swarm optimization (PSO) [272], ant colony
optimization (ACO) [149], and related swarm intelligence methods embody this approach.
     Distributed optimization can be traced back to early studies on decentralized control
and parallel processing in the 1970s and 1980s [504]. Over time, as networking technolo-
gies matured, applications of distributed optimization proliferated in sensor networks,
communication systems, and large-scale machine learning (e.g., federated learning). Mean-
while, swarm intelligence took shape in the 1990s with foundational algorithms such as
particle swarm optimization by Kennedy and Eberhart [272] and ant colony optimization by
Dorigo and co-authors [149]. These bio-inspired algorithms emphasize simplicity, adapt-
ability, and the emergent behavior arising from the coordination of many simple agents.
     Both distributed and swarm approaches can be viewed as leveraging the idea of collec-
tive decision-making to handle large, complex problems. Distributed optimization typically
focuses on rigorous mathematical decomposition, while swarm methods favor iterative
search processes guided by heuristics and emergent coordination.

Distributed Optimization
A common scenario in distributed optimization arises when one has a large-scale objective

make the scenario more realistic and robust.
     The alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) is one popular choice to de-

efficient parallelism. Because this approach decomposes large problems across multiple

Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)
Swarm optimization methods view a population of agents (e.g., particles, ants, bees) that
each maintaining a candidate solution. The overall solution quality emerges from how
these agents interact and update themselves based on shared experience or local heuris-
tics [272, 57]. Swarm optimization, in contrast to distributed optimization, is relatively
easy to implement and is versatile for many black-box or nonsmooth problems. It relies
on global search heuristics (e.g., swarm intelligence) to escape local minima in noncon-
vex problems. Like distributed optimization, it is parallelizable, as swarm updates can be
computed in parallel for each agent.
    There are no exact guarantees for swarm optimization, as the heuristics can fail to
find global optima or converge slowly. The performance is also sensitive to hyperparam-
eter tuning. Finally, the algorithm scaling is not always favorable, as it may require large
swarms and many iterations to converge in high-dimensional space.

space. The algorithm updates these based on the best known position the particle has
individually found and the best known position found by any particle:

Constraints and Duality

Constraints are an essential component of many optimization problems. Most real-world
problems are constrained by resources, time, material properties, cost, etc. Until now we
have mostly either considered unconstrained problems, such as least-squares regression
and gradient descent, or problems with the simplest constraints and cost functions, such
as linear programming.
     Constraints shape the feasible set of an optimization problem. In Chapter 3 on linear
programming, we saw that the constraints almost entirely determine the problem, as the
optimum always lives on the vertex of the feasible polytope defined by the constraints.
We also saw constraints arise in Chapter 4 in the context of constrained linear regression.
The importance of constraints in optimization extends far beyond these simple linear and
quadratic problems, becoming critical for modeling a wide range of real-world systems.
     We begin this chapter with an in-depth discussion about Lagrange multipliers for
enforcing equality constraints. We will derive the conditions for optimality of a con-
strained problem, with several examples. Next, we formally introduce the more general
Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions, which hold for inequality constraints as well. La-
grange multipliers and the KKT conditions have an intuitive geometric interpretation in
terms of directional derivatives of the objective and constraint functions. Specifically, lo-
cally optimal solutions will be those such that the gradient of the objective function has
no direction tangent to the constraint equations. Thus, the objective normal must be in the
span of the constraint normal directions, and the linear combination of these constraint
normals will be the Lagrange multipliers.
     The interactions between constraints and the objective function are surprisingly rich
and interesting. The concept of duality provides crucial insight into this connection. At
its core, duality provides a deeper perspective on constrained optimization, often by refor-
mulating the original, primal problem into a complementary dual problem. Analyzing this
dual problem can reveal tight bounds on the original objective and provide methods for
efficient computation. In some cases, the dual formulation may be simpler or more com-
putationally tractable. We will explore duality and constraints, expanding and developing
the earlier perspective in Section 1.4 based on infinite penalty functions. Dual variables
also have the useful interpretation as the sensitivities of the objective function with respect
to active constraints.
     Duality is most easily understood through linear programming (via the primal-dual
pair of linear programs) and quadratic programming (via the classical use of Lagrange

                                             203
204                                                CHAPTER 6. CONSTRAINTS AND DUALITY


multipliers and KKT conditions). However, the concept of duality is much more widely
applicable. In mechanics and physics, for instance, dual principles connect variational for-
mulations of equilibrium conditions with complementary energy or stress-based descrip-
tions. In the calculus of variations, dual arguments can simplify or solve certain boundary
value problems by re-expressing constraints or re-labelling the roles of independent and
dependent variables.
    Duality then lead us to interior point methods (IPMs), which are a powerful class of
algorithms for constrained optimization. There are several variants of IPMs that leverage
primal and dual formulations to efficiently find optimal solutions to convex problems.
These methods may also be used for nonconvex problems, as were discussed in Chapter 5,
although there are typically fewer guarantees in the nonconvex case, and extra care must
be taken.
    Next, we introduce one of the most widely used splitting algorithms, the alternating
direction method of multipliers (ADMM). ADMM is a powerful primal-dual method that
is used to handle complex problems with nonsmooth regularizers. ADMM relies on the
proximal operator from Sec. 5.1, and is closely related to the sparse, relaxed, regularized
regression from the same section. Both methods involve operator splitting and proximal
steps; however, SR3 is primal only, while ADMM is primal-dual. This introduces subtle
differences that we will discuss throughout this section.
    Finally, we will introduce infinite dimensional optimization problems and the calculus
of variations. This is a rich field with an equally rich history. Although it may seem beyond
the scope of this chapter, the concepts of constraints and duality were first introduced
in these infinite-dimensional problems in the context of classical mechanics. The Euler-
Lagrange equations, which are used to derive the equations of motion in physics, also
provide optimality conditions for infinite-dimensional optimization problems. Further,
the Legendre transformation is an important transformation of the Lagrangian into a dual
Hamiltonian formulation.
    Variational optimization is extremely relevant for broad and useful classes of infinite
dimensional problems, including control theory, machine learning, and shape optimiza-
tion in fluid applications. We will investigate several key infinite dimensional optimiza-
tion problems, such as the classic Brachistochrone and Tautochrone problems, which have
broad implications for modern optimization problems. We already saw a very simplified
cousin of the Brachistochrone problem in Example Panel 1.2 in Chapter 1, and we will
also explore how to solve the full Brachistochrone problem using modern techniques in
automatic differentiation in Chapter 10.
    To summarize, this chapter will accomplish three major goals:

  1. Describe how to handle constraints, and how they may be tractably optimized through
     Lagrange multipliers and KKT conditions;

  2. Introduce the related concept of duality, which is important in advanced algorithms,
     such as IPMs, discussed in Sec. 6.4, and ADMM, discussed in Sec. 6.5;

  3. Discuss infinite-dimensional problems where the objective is a functional of a func-
     tion to be optimized.

Panel 6.1: Minimax Inequality
   To derive the general form of the minimax inequality, which we used in Eq. (6.3),
   we let f : X × Y → R be any real-valued function. Then we have

                             min max     f (x, y)    ≥    max min      f (x, y).
                             x∈X y∈Y                       y∈Y x∈X

   This inequality always holds, regardless of whether f is continuous or convex. It
   reflects the general fact that min and max do not commute.
   This may be derived using the following pointwise inequality, which is true for all
   x ∈ X and y ∈ Y :

                                min f (x′ , y) ≤ f (x, y) ≤ max f (x, y ′ ).
                                x′ ∈X                        ′
                                                             y ∈Y

   Taking maxy on the left and minx on the right yields the desired minimax inequality:

                             max min     f (x, y)    ≤    min max      f (x, y).
                             y∈Y x∈X                      x∈X y∈Y

   This is often discussed in the context of zero-sum game theory. In zero-sum games,
   f (x, y) represents the payoff function for Player A choosing x and Player B choosing
   y. Then minx maxy f (x, y) is the best worst-case outcome Player A can guarantee,
   while maxy minx f (x, y) is the best guaranteed outcome Player B can ensure. The
   minimax inequality reflects that, in general, the first player is at a disadvantage,
   since they commit to a strategy without knowledge of the other’s move.

Geometry of Lagrange Multipliers

Geometrically, for x∗ to be an optimal solution, the gradient of f (x∗ ) can have no compo-
nent tangent to h(x) = 0 at x∗ . A tangent direction d to h(x) = 0 satisfies ∇h(x) · d = 0.
These tangent directions are feasible directions in which we can move the variable x to
improve the objective function while still maintaining the constraint equation. If ∇f im-
proves in one of these feasible tangent directions, then the current point is not optimal.
Thus, ∇f must be normal to h(x), meaning that it is in the span of the normals ∇hj (x).
    This is simple to visualize for a single constraint h(x) = 0 in two-dimensions, x ∈ R2 ,
as in Fig. 6.1. If f (x) decreases in a direction that is tangent to h, meaning that ∇f (x)
has a component tangent to h, then we can achieve a solution that is closer to optimal
by moving in this direction. Thus, a necessary condition for optimality is that ∇f (x) is
orthogonal to the tangent space at h, which means it is in the direction of ∇h. We will
revisit and generalize this geometric intuition to higher dimensions and more constraints
soon.

Regularity Conditions
The use of Lagrange multipliers requires a mild set of regularity conditions, called con-
straint qualifications, to ensure the KKT conditions hold at a solution. These conditions
rule out pathological cases, such as when the active constraints at a point are not linearly
independent or when the feasible region has no interior.
    For equality-constrained problems, a key regularity condition is that gradients of the
active constraints must be linearly independent at x∗ , so the Jacobian ∇h(x∗ ) has full rank:
                                                           
                            rank ∇h1 (x∗ ) . . . ∇hm (x∗ ) = m.

This ensures that the set of feasible directions (i.e., the tangent space to the constraint sur-
face) has the expected dimension. If the constraint gradients are linearly dependent, then
instead of intersecting transversely to form a well-behaved surface, the constraints may
define a degenerate feasible region. Thus, there may not be Lagrange multipliers that sat-
isfy the stationarity condition, even at a point that minimizes the objective and satisfies the
constraints. For example, two equality constraints in 2D whose gradients point in the same
direction define parallel lines so the feasible region may be empty or ill-defined. Similar
regularity conditions exist for the more general KKT theory of inequality constraints.

History
Lagrange first introduced the method of multipliers to eliminate constraint forces in holo-
nomic3 mechanics [294, 332]. Figure 6.5 shows a simple mechanical system of a block slid-
ing down an inclined plane, where the plane exerts a force to keep the block exactly on the
boundary; this force is a Lagrange multiplier enforcing the constraint that the block remains
exactly on the boundary of the inclined plane. We will discuss the broader connections of
constraints, Lagrange multipliers, and classical mechanics in Section 6.6.
    Nearly two hundred years later, this approach was generalized to handle inequality
constraints by Karush [270] and popularized by Kuhn and Tucker [290], marking the birth
of modern nonlinear programming. Since then, the field has developed rapidly, with the
theory of duality and the primal-dual algorithms that followed.
   3
    A holonomic constraint is a constraint on a mechanical system that can be expressed as an explicit equation
relating the generalized coordinates, typically in the form h(q1 , q2 , . . . , qn , t) = 0. Such constraints restrict
the configuration space but not the velocities independently. In contrast, nonholonomic constraints involve
the velocities and cannot be integrated to purely positional constraints. For example, a coin rolling without
slipping is a non-holonomic system, while a block sliding down an incline is a holonomic system.

dual objective will have a term µT g(x), so if any µj is negative, then multiplying with any
inactive negative constraints gj (x) < 0, we can drive this maximization to infinity. Thus,

Algorithmic Perspectives
The KKT system of equations underpin many of the most powerful modern algorithms for
constrained optimization, including active-set, interior-point, and augmented Lagrangian
methods. These algorithms are closely related to the notion of duality, and we will discuss
them more in this Chapter and in Chapter 5. We provide a brief overview here:

  (a) Active-Set Methods (ASM) and Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP). Active
      set methods involve guessing which inequality constraints are active and become
      equality constraints. Then the unconstrained Lagrangian in Eq. (6.2) is solved, for
      example with Newton’s method. Finally, constraints are tested to see if they enter or
      exit after this iteration. In sequential quadratic programming, we approximate f (x)
      with a quadratic.

 (b) Interior-Point Methods (IPM). IPMs attempt to iteratively solve for an optimal point
     (x∗ , λ∗ , µ∗ ) that satisfy the KKT conditions. We replace each gj (x) ≤ 0 in the La-
     grangian objective function with a log-barrier function −t log(−gj (x)). A Newton it-
     eration on the barrier-augmented objective function will enforce gj (x) ≤ 0. As t → 0,
     the solution converges to a KKT point. We will explore IPMs in detail in Section 6.4.

  (c) Augmented Lagrangian Methods (ALM). Here we rewrite our inequality constraints
      g(x) ≤ 0 as equality constraints by introducing slack variables: g(x)+s = 0 with s ≥
      0. We then write the augmented Lagrangian to include penalty terms ρ2 ∥g(x) + s∥2 to

Regularity Conditions
There are regularity conditions, known as constraint qualifications, for when a constrained
optimizer x∗ will satisfy the KKT conditions. The regularity conditions are more compli-
cated in the case of mixed equality and inequality constraints than those for simple equality
constraints. In this case, several constraint qualifications can be used:
   • Slater’s Condition. For convex problems, Slater’s condition is a powerful and prac-
     tical regularity test. It simply requires the existence of a strictly feasible point x0 :
                                      h(x0 ) = 0     and g(x0 ) < 0
     so that all inequality and equality constraints are satisfied strictly at x0 . This must
     only be true at a single feasible x0 , and gives a global result. Slater’s condition guar-
     antees strong duality and ensures the KKT conditions are necessary and sufficient for
     optimality in convex problems. For linear programs, Slater’s condition automatically
     holds if the feasible set has a non-empty interior. For nonconvex problems, Slater’s
     condition does not guarantee strong duality or the sufficiency of the KKT conditions.
     It may still ensure that the KKT conditions are necessary for optimality, but only if
     other constraint qualifications, such as LICQ or MFCQ, are also satisfied.
   • Linear Independence Constraint Qualification (LICQ). This is one of the most com-
     monly used regularity conditions. As before, the gradients of all active constraints
     (i.e., equality constraints h(x) = 0 and inequality constraints gj (x∗ ) = 0 active at x∗ )
     must be linearly independent. LICQ ensures that if x∗ is a local minimizer, then there
     exist unique Lagrange multipliers (λ, µ) that satisfy the KKT conditions. LICQ alone
     does not ensure existence of an optimizer or sufficiency of KKT. In the convex case,
     LICQ is stronger than Slater for existence and isn’t needed for sufficiency.
   • Mangasarian–Fromovitz Constraint Qualification (MFCQ). This is a bit weaker than
     LICQ and states that the equality constraint gradients ∇h(x∗ ) are linearly indepen-
     dent, and that there exists a direction d ∈ Rn such that:
                  ∇hi (x∗ ) · d = 0 for all i,        ∇gj (x∗ ) · d < 0 for all active j.
     This guarantees the existence of a feasible direction that moves away from the active
     inequality constraints. Unlike LICQ, MFCQ allows for linear dependence among
     active inequalities, as long as there is a direction of descent that maintains feasibility.

6.3      Duality
Duality is one of the most profound and useful concepts in optimization. For every con-
strained optimization problem, there is a dual optimization problem that provides a lower
bound to the optimum of the original primal problem. We have already seen a simple ex-
ample of a dual optimization problem in Chapter 3 on linear programming. These ideas
generalize naturally to other convex and nonconvex optimization problems. Regardless of
the convexity of the primal problem, the dual problem is always concave.
    The dual optimization problem is closely related to Lagrange multipliers and the KKT
conditions. We will show that the Lagrange multipliers λ and µ are actually decision vari-
ables for the dual problem. When only equality constraints are present, this dual problem
is unconstrained. For a mix of equality and inequality constraints, the dual has positivity
constraints on the Lagrange multipliers µ corresponding to the inequality constraints.
    Primal–dual interior-point methods are a class of algorithms for modern high-dimensional
constrained optimization, discussed in Section 6.4 Duality is also important in the alter-
nating direction method of multipliers (ADMM), discussed in Section 6.5, and branch-
and-bound algorithms for combinatorial and nonconvex problems. These approaches are
central for many problems we have already seen, such as box-constrained least squares,
LASSO, and ridge regression. When the dual equals the primal, then no further improve-
ment is possible, providing a certificate of optimality.

                                        max ϕ(λ, µ) ≤ min f (x).                                        (6.22)

    This is an extremely useful property for algorithms and optimality certificates. Specif-
ically, the dual variables (i.e., the Lagrange multipliers (λ, µ)) allow us to apply pressure

lower bound. The dual function measures how low the objective function can be pushed
down if constraint violations are allowed but penalized. Thus, dual solutions give safe
lower bounds on the primal problem, even if infeasible points are allowed.
    There is an even more fundamental property of the primal-dual relationship that even
holds for non-optimal points. Weak duality always holds, and states that for any feasible
primal x ∈ S and any feasible dual (λ, µ), then

                                             ϕ(λ, µ) ≤ f (x).                                           (6.23)

Thus, the value of any dual feasible point is a lower bound on the value of any primal
feasible point. This is always true, even for nonconvex problems.

duality holds for convex problems under mild regularity conditions, such as Slater’s con-
dition, namely the existence of a strictly feasible point. Under these mild assumptions,
strong duality states that the primal and dual optimum values are equal to each other9 :

                                            ϕ(λ∗ , µ∗ ) = f (x∗ ).                                      (6.24)

For nonconvex problems strong duality might not hold and there may be a duality gap.
    Dual variables are also important to quantify the sensitivity of the optimum to pertur-
bations in the constraint right-hand sides. When the necessary conditions are met, dual
variables are the partial derivatives of the optimal value with respect to constraint right-
hand sides. This connection bridges optimization theory with sensitivity analysis and sup-
ports the practical use of duality in engineering, economics, and control.

Intuition Behind the Dual Linear Program. It is helpful to discuss the intuition behind
the dual form of a linear program, as this is the simplest case to understand, and it will
provide a basis for more complex primal-dual problems and algorithms.
    Given a maximization maxx≥0 cT x subject to the constraints Ax ≤ b, the idea is to try
and provide the tightest upper bound on cT x by taking linear combinations of the con-
straint equations aj x ≤ bj . The coefficients of the linear combinations of these equations
will be denoted by y, which we saw above are the dual variables and the Lagrange multi-
pliers enforcing the constraints. We know that any feasible solution will satisfy all of these
constraints, so any linear combination will also be satisfied for any feasible point. This
linear combination of the constraints yields the following equation:
                                                yT Ax ≤ yT b.                                           (6.34)
Thus, the product yT A provides the coefficients of x. If these coefficients are greater than
or equal to cT , then yT b provides a direct upper bound on cT x; note that this relies on
x ≥ 0, although this condition may be relaxed. This means that for any yT A ≥ cT , then
yT b will provide an upper bound on the primal. Since we seek the tightest bound, we
minimize yT b over y ≥ 0 such that yT A ≥ cT . This is shown schematically in Fig. 6.8.
